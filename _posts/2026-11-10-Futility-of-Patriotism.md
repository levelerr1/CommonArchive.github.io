> At What Cost? The Heavy Price of Loving Nigeria

We all want to walk towards a better Nigeria. We are willing to give it our all; our energy, our time, our intellect, 
our passion. But at what cost?

Look at the trail we’ve left behind. Lives lost in the heat of passion, in the heat of patriotism, in the sheer desperate
hunger for a functional country. Young Nigerians lost their lives. Promising futures cut short. Breadwinners gone. Only 
children taken from parents who had invested everything into raising them. People who were once called *mothers* have 
been forced into the agonizing position of becoming barren not because they couldn't have children, but because the children
they poured their lives into were snatched away, all for the hope that Nigeria could finally work. And has it gotten better?
No. It has only gotten worse.

So now, who wants to sacrifice their life for a Nigeria that may not even turn out better, despite laying down everything? 
Nobody wants to be *Jesus Christ* for Nigeria. Especially not when that ultimate sacrifice isn't going to be recognized, nor 
will it ever be compensated. If anything at all, those who die fighting for change aren't honored; they are whispered about 
as the *foolish* ones who didn't know any better.

We want a better nation, yes. But our parents have invested too much in us, and we refuse to waste their hope. Patriotism 
shouldn't be a death sentence. 

*"At what cost?"*

The idea of the *patriotic Nigerian* has gradually morphed into a joke, a fantasy. Over the years we have had a heroes who have
advocated for the growth of the nation and fought injustice. One of our national heroes that I will emphasize on today is *Fela 
Anikulapo Kuti(1938-1997)*. He was born in the former British-Nigeria regime and grew up experiencing the inequalities Nigerians
faced before and after gaining independence. He is a man who can best be described as “Patriotism comes at a cost”. As a person 
who has experienced the heights of discrimination and the remnant of the colonial rigidness, he openly opposed the Nigerian 
government for its blatant abuse of power and enabling policies.  These actions would lead to significant actions consisting of, 
but not limited to, his incarceration on *September* 4, 1984 (*Amnesty.org, January 1985*) and the battery on his mother by the
Nigerian military on February 18, 1977 (*Tayo Agunbiade via Aljazeera, October 2020*). From his arts, we will isolate three 
components he insists are responsible for the decline of Nigeria. 

The first issue he identifies is the *colonial interests* and its disadvantages to the Nigerian citizen. In his song *Colonial mentality*,
and *I.T.T*, he insists that the European influences taints the ingenuity, culture and creativity of the African man. His arguments were 
hardly centered on racism, but rather the indoctrination of the people by western influences. In his song *I.T.T*, he narrates how people
jeopardize their countries for financial gains from foreigners and other authoritative figures.

![Fela]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/fela.jpeg)

The people is the second idea *Fela* argues. He insists that the native man’s habits enable the destruction of his social structure and 
is the cause of catastrophic infrastructural damage. This is a central idea in his song *Suffering and Smiling*, he sings about the 
tolerating nature of Nigerians —which is an aspect of our culture by the way— and the resistance to revolution.

Lastly, the most popular ideology of the great singer is *The Government*. During his years in entertainment, *Fela* has had multiple
encounters with the Nigerian government, physically and non-physically. He strongly maintains that the other two issues mentioned above 
are exponentially amplified by the poor governmental bureaucracy existing. *Beast of no nation* narrates his friction with the Nigerian 
military and the failing judiciary. In all, the arguments of the great singer *Fela Anilkulapo Kuti* is still relevant 29 years after 
his death.
